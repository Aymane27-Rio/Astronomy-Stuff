A FIELD GUIDE TO THE NIGHT SKY

Read the sky.
Find the patterns.

Planets wander along a shared path. Stars sketch familiar shapes across the night. Learn what those patterns mean—and how to find a few for yourself.

01 / THE PLANETS FROM EARTH

A lineup is a point of view

The planets can appear strung out across the sky, but they are still separated by vast distances. Their orbits lie close to a flat plane; from Earth, that plane looks like a path across the sky called the ecliptic.

01

Alignment

A casual term for planets appearing along the same stretch of sky. They usually form an arc, not a ruler-straight line.

02

Conjunction

Two celestial objects appear especially close together from our viewpoint. They may still be millions of kilometres apart.

03

Not a rare cosmic force

A sky lineup does not pull planets together or cause extraordinary effects on Earth. It is a geometry-and-viewpoint event.

SAME SKY · DIFFERENT DISTANCES

Our solar system from above

The planets travel on separate orbits around the Sun. Because those orbits are nearly in one plane, their apparent positions trace a similar route across our sky.

Naked-eye planets: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn can be seen without a telescope when their positions and sky conditions cooperate. Check a current sky chart for your location—and never look directly at the Sun to find a nearby planet.

02 / TWO FAMOUS ASTERISMS

The Big Dipper points north

The Big and Little Dippers are recognizable star patterns called asterisms. Each sits inside a larger official constellation: Ursa Major, the Great Bear, and Ursa Minor, the Little Bear.

THE BIG DIPPER

Seven bright stars make the familiar bowl and handle. The pattern is only part of the much larger Ursa Major constellation.

THE LITTLE DIPPER

Polaris, the North Star, marks the end of its handle. The rest of this smaller pattern is dimmer and can be harder to spot.

A WAYFINDING CLUE

Dubhe and Merak are the “pointer stars.” Trace from Merak through Dubhe and extend the line to find Polaris.

Remember: Polaris is not the brightest star in the night sky. It appears nearly aligned with Earth’s north rotational axis, so the northern sky seems to turn around it.

03 / MORE WAYS TO TRACE THE SKY

Constellations, asterisms, and clusters

The lines on a star chart are guides we draw between stars. Most stars in a pattern are at very different distances and are not physically connected. The IAU defines 88 official constellation regions; cultures also share many different star patterns and stories.

CONSTELLATION

Orion

Find the three bright stars in Orion’s Belt. They sit between the reddish Betelgeuse and blue-white Rigel in the familiar hunter pattern.

CONSTELLATION

Cassiopeia

Five bright stars form a distinctive W or M, depending on its orientation. In much of the northern hemisphere it circles the pole opposite the Big Dipper.

ASTERISM

Summer Triangle

A large sky pattern joining Vega, Deneb, and Altair—the brightest stars in Lyra, Cygnus, and Aquila. It crosses three separate constellations.

OPEN STAR CLUSTER

The Pleiades

A real family of young stars in Taurus, also known as the Seven Sisters. Its compact blue-white grouping is different from a stick-figure constellation.
